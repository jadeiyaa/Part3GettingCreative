The research behind Sam

Sam isn't real, unfortunately. But, I made him up to turn a semester of research into something visible rather than some pile of stats. Here's what it's really built on. This is a young, still-developing area of research, so I don't think any one study here is the final word.

Diet & mood link

Jafari et al., 2026 — 263 teen girls in Tehran; more ultra-processed food was tied to meaningfully higher odds of depression, stress, and anxiety, even after accounting for BMI and activity. It's survey-based, so I'd call this an association worth taking seriously, not proof.

Correlation isn't causation

Winpenny et al., 2018 — 603 UK teens (the ROOTS cohort). A similar diet–depression link mostly disappeared once smoking and alcohol use were controlled for, and was gone completely by a later check-in. This is the study that made me rethink the first one.

Access & income

Dunford et al., 2025 — ~33 million grocery purchases across almost 60,000 U.S. households. Lower-income, lower-education groups bought a modestly higher, statistically significant share of ultra-processed food.

Why income might matter

Pereira et al., 2026 — 128 families in rural riverside communities in Brazil. Lower income predicted more food insecurity, and it was the food insecurity — not income on its own — that predicted a harder quality of life for the kids.

A possible mechanism

Dr. Uma Naidoo, via Aujla, 2022 — On The Doctor's Kitchen podcast, nutritional psychiatrist Dr. Uma Naidoo talks about "psychobiotics": gut bacteria, shaped by diet, potentially influencing mood alongside (not instead of) other mental health care.
